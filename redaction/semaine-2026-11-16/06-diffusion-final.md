On 10 November in Lagos, Nigeria's finance minister, Taiwo Oyedele, proposed linking the bond markets of Nigeria, Ghana and the eight WAEMU countries, with an issuer passport, a single investor identifier, connected settlement systems and a regional bond index.

The plan treats regulatory fragmentation as the main obstacle, while yields point to the currency. At ten years, Nigeria's federal government pays 17.39% in naira and Côte d'Ivoire borrows at 6.25% in CFA francs. From 2015 to 2025, consumer prices rose by 17.7% a year on average in Nigeria and by 2.2% in Côte d'Ivoire. For a bank in Abidjan, the extra eleven points barely compensate for the depreciation such an inflation gap tends to bring, and no passport removes that risk.

WAEMU's exchange-control rules also require banks to fund at least 75% of their purchases of authorised foreign securities with resources raised abroad. And a third of WAMU government securities outstanding is issued by Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger, which have left ECOWAS, the framework in which the whole integration architecture has been built since 2013.

A more realistic sequence would start with what does not depend on the currency. Nigeria could finance shared settlement and custody infrastructure, companies and development banks could borrow in the currency of their revenues, and Accra and Abidjan could build a first link.

The Central Bank of Nigeria's Monetary Policy Committee meets on 23 and 24 November, a first signal on the disinflation the rest depends on.
